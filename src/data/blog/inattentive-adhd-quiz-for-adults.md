If you've ever taken a standard **ADHD quiz for adults** and scored low despite losing your keys weekly, zoning out mid-conversation, and drowning in half-finished projects, you've likely run into a real gap in how most screeners are built. Many popular quizzes weight hyperactive and impulsive items heavily, so if your ADHD shows up as quiet, internal, and easy to mistake for "just tired" or "a bit scattered," you can score well below the cutoff while still meeting every clinical criterion for **inattentive ADHD**.

This quiz uses a different tool: the nine **inattentive-presentation criteria from the DSM-5**, the same list clinicians reference when they suspect ADHD isn't showing up as the stereotypical bouncing-off-the-walls hyperactivity.

### What Inattentive ADHD Actually Looks Like in Adults

Inattentive ADHD in adults rarely looks like the "can't sit still" stereotype most people picture. It looks like starting five tasks and finishing none, re-reading the same paragraph four times, missing details in emails you skimmed too fast, and losing your train of thought mid-sentence in meetings. There's no visible fidgeting or interrupting to tip anyone off, which is exactly why this presentation goes unrecognized for decades, especially in people who were quiet, "dreamy," or high-achieving as kids.

Clinically, this is called the **predominantly inattentive presentation** of ADHD, one of three DSM-5 presentation types alongside hyperactive-impulsive and combined. Our [clinical landscape overview](/posts/understanding-the-clinical-landscape-of-adhd-a-comprehensive-overview/) breaks down how all three differ if you want the fuller picture.

### The Inattentive ADHD Quiz for Adults: 9 DSM-5 Criteria

For each item, be honest about how often it happens **now**, not how you wish you behaved. Mark it "yes" only if it happens often enough to noticeably affect your work, relationships, or daily functioning.

1. I often fail to give close attention to details, or I make careless mistakes in work or other tasks.
2. I often have trouble holding attention during tasks or conversations, even ones I care about.
3. I often don't seem to listen when spoken to directly, even when I'm trying to.
4. I often don't follow through on instructions and fail to finish work, chores, or duties, not from defiance but from losing track.
5. I often have trouble organizing tasks and activities — messy work, poor time management, missed deadlines.
6. I often avoid or dread tasks that require sustained mental effort, like paperwork, forms, or long reports.
7. I often lose things needed for tasks, like keys, phone, wallet, or documents.
8. I'm often easily distracted by unrelated thoughts or things happening around me.
9. I'm often forgetful in daily activities, like appointments, chores, or returning calls.

**Scoring:** Count how many items you marked "yes."

| Score | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| 0–2 | Inattentive ADHD is unlikely, though a rough patch or high stress can temporarily mimic a couple of these. |
| 3–4 | A meaningful number of symptoms; worth tracking over the next few weeks and revisiting. |
| 5 or more | This meets the DSM-5 adult threshold for the inattentive presentation and is a strong reason to pursue a full evaluation. |

The DSM-5 threshold for adults (age 17+) is **five or more of the nine symptoms**, present in two or more settings (home, work, relationships), persisting for at least six months, with some symptoms present before age 12, even if they weren't recognized as ADHD at the time.

### Why Standard ADHD Quizzes Miss Inattentive Symptoms

Most short adult ADHD screeners, including the widely used ASRS-6, include six items pulled from all eighteen DSM-5 criteria, and only four of those six are inattentive items. Two are hyperactive-impulsive items about fidgeting and feeling "driven by a motor." If your ADHD has zero hyperactive component, a fixed one-third weighting toward symptoms you don't have can pull your total score below the screener's cutoff, even when your inattentive symptoms alone are severe.

This is a known limitation, not a flaw unique to any one quiz: brief screeners trade completeness for speed, and that trade-off systematically disadvantages people whose ADHD doesn't include a hyperactive-impulsive component. It's the same mechanism, applied to presentation type instead of gender, that causes many women to score low on standard tools, which we cover in more depth in our [ADHD test for women guide](/posts/adhd-test-for-women-self-screening-guide/).

### Signs of Inattentive ADHD That Aren't on Any Checklist

Beyond the nine formal criteria, these patterns show up constantly in adults with inattentive ADHD but rarely make it into a quiz:

- **"Cotton brain"** — a foggy, half-tuned-in feeling during ordinary tasks, distinct from actual daydreaming about something specific.
- **Rereading without absorbing** — your eyes move across a page or screen, but nothing lands, so you circle back and reread the same section.
- **Chronic time blindness** — misjudging how long tasks take by wide margins, which we unpack fully in [the mystery of time blindness](/posts/the-mystery-of-time-blindness-why-the-adhd-brain-struggles-with-the-future/).
- **Decision fatigue by early afternoon** — sustained attention drains faster than it seems to for peers, leaving little left for evening decisions.
- **A childhood reputation as "smart but spacey"** — decent grades built on last-minute focus, alongside constant comments about not paying attention.

**Pro-tip from ADHD m8**: If three or more of these feel uncomfortably familiar but your quiz score landed at 3 or 4, don't round down to "probably fine." Borderline scores on a 9-item list are still real data.

### FAQ: Inattentive ADHD Quiz Questions

**Is inattentive ADHD the same as ADD?**
"ADD" was the term used before the DSM-IV merged attention-deficit disorder and hyperactivity disorder into a single diagnosis, ADHD, with three presentation types. When people search "ADD quiz," they're almost always describing what clinicians now call the inattentive presentation of ADHD.

**Can inattentive ADHD turn into combined-type ADHD later?**
Presentation type can shift over a lifetime as circumstances change, most commonly because hyperactivity in children tends to look more like inner restlessness in adults, which can make a childhood combined-type case look inattentive-only by adulthood, or vice versa.

**Why did I score low on a normal ADHD quiz but high on this one?**
Standard screeners like the ASRS-6 sample only a few items from all eighteen DSM-5 criteria and include hyperactive-impulsive items that don't apply if your ADHD is purely inattentive, which can pull your score below the cutoff despite real, significant symptoms.
