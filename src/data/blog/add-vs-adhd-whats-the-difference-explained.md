**ADD vs ADHD** is one of the most common questions people type into Google, and the short answer is that there is no difference anymore. ADD is an outdated name that is now covered by ADHD. What most people mean by "ADD" is the inattentive presentation of ADHD.

This guide explains where the term ADD came from, why the name changed, what the three modern ADHD presentations look like in adults, and what it all means if you are chasing a diagnosis.

> **Pro-tip from ADHD m8:** If a doctor, a relative or a TikTok says "I have ADD, not ADHD", they are not wrong, just using the 1980s label. Don't waste energy arguing the terminology. Describe the symptoms instead.

_This post is general information, not medical advice. Only a qualified clinician can diagnose ADHD._

---

### Is ADD the Same as ADHD?

ADD and ADHD are the same condition, and ADHD is the only correct current name. "ADD" survives in everyday speech, but it is no longer a separate diagnosis in any major medical manual.

The [American Psychiatric Association](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/adhd/what-is-adhd) describes ADHD as a single neurodevelopmental condition marked by a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity. The [NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd) uses the same framing. Neither lists "ADD" as its own disorder.

So if you have been told you "only" have ADD, what that really means is that you have ADHD without obvious hyperactivity.

---

### Why Was It Called ADD in the First Place?

The label ADD came from the 1980 edition of the diagnostic manual (DSM-III), which split the condition into "ADD with hyperactivity" and "ADD without hyperactivity". The name then changed to ADHD in 1987 and has stayed that way since.

Here is the short timeline:

- **1980 (DSM-III)**: "Attention Deficit Disorder", with or without hyperactivity
- **1987 (DSM-III-R)**: renamed "Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder", with a single list of symptoms
- **1994 (DSM-IV)**: three subtypes introduced: inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive and combined
- **2013 (DSM-5)**: "subtypes" renamed "presentations", and symptoms re-worded for adults

The old name stuck because it was popular for a long time, and because it felt kinder than a label with "hyperactivity" in it. That is also why many adults who were diagnosed in the 1990s still say ADD.

### What Is Inattentive ADHD (the Thing People Call ADD)?

Inattentive ADHD is the presentation where attention, organisation and memory problems dominate, with little outward hyperactivity. It is what most people picture when they say "ADD", and it is the presentation most often missed in adults, girls and women.

Typical signs of inattentive ADHD in adults include:

- Losing keys, phone and wallet on a daily basis
- Starting tasks and drifting off halfway through
- Zoning out in conversations or meetings
- Chronic lateness and missed deadlines
- Feeling mentally foggy or "slow" even when you are smart

Because nobody is bouncing off the walls, inattentive ADHD is often dismissed as laziness, daydreaming or anxiety. ### What Are the Three ADHD Presentations?

ADHD has three official presentations: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined. Your presentation is decided by which symptom group meets the diagnostic threshold, and it can change over a lifetime.

| Presentation                        | Main pattern                                | What it can look like in adults                       |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Predominantly inattentive           | Attention, memory and organisation problems | Fog, forgetfulness, unfinished projects               |
| Predominantly hyperactive-impulsive | Restlessness and impulsivity                | Inner restlessness, interrupting, impulsive decisions |
| Combined                            | Both groups of symptoms                     | Distractible and restless and impulsive               |

For adults and teens aged 17 and over, the DSM-5 requires at least **five** symptoms in a group for that group to count. Children need six. Symptoms must also show up in more than one setting, such as work and home, and must have been present before age 12. The [CDC's overview of ADHD](https://www.cdc.gov/adhd/about/index.html) lays out these criteria in plain language.

### Can Your ADHD Presentation Change Over Time?

Yes, ADHD presentation can change across life. Hyperactivity often softens with age and turns into inner restlessness, which is why a hyperactive child can become an adult who looks mostly inattentive.

That is why the adult version of "ADD" is so common. A child who was a whirlwind at age 8 can be a quiet, overwhelmed 35-year-old who is constantly mentally busy. The underlying ADHD has not gone away. It has just changed how it shows up. ### Does the Label Change Treatment?

The presentation label matters less than your actual symptoms and daily impact. Treatment for ADHD, whether inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive or combined, is based on the same toolkit: medication, behavioural strategies, coaching and environment changes.

What is usually different is the target. Inattentive ADHD often needs more support with organisation, memory and task initiation. Hyperactive-impulsive ADHD often needs more help with impulse control and emotional regulation. ### FAQ: ADD vs ADHD

**Q:** Is ADD a real diagnosis today?
A: No. ADD is a retired term. The current diagnosis is ADHD, with a "predominantly inattentive presentation" if hyperactivity is not prominent.

**Q:** Which is worse, ADD or ADHD?
A: Neither is worse as a category. Severity depends on how much symptoms disrupt your work, relationships and wellbeing, not on the presentation label.

**Q:** Can you have ADHD without being hyperactive?
A: Yes. The predominantly inattentive presentation has no requirement for hyperactivity, and many adults with ADHD describe only an internal, mental restlessness.

**Q:** Why do doctors still say ADD sometimes?
A: Habit. Many clinicians trained when ADD was the official label, and patients recognise the term. It is harmless in conversation, but diagnostic reports should say ADHD.

**Q:** How do I get assessed for ADHD as an adult?
A: Start with a screening tool, then book an assessment with a psychiatrist or psychologist experienced in adult ADHD. Our [adult ADHD diagnosis guide](/posts/adult-adhd-diagnosis-guide) walks through the process step by step.

### Key Takeaways

- ADD and ADHD are the same condition, and ADHD is the only current official name.
- "ADD" came from the 1980 DSM-III and was replaced by "ADHD" in the 1987 DSM-III-R.
- What people call ADD is now called the predominantly inattentive presentation of ADHD.
- ADHD has three presentations (inattentive, hyperactive-impulsive and combined), and adults need five symptoms in a group to meet the DSM-5 threshold.
- A person's ADHD presentation can change over a lifetime, with hyperactivity often shifting to inner restlessness in adulthood.

---

### Conclusion

ADD versus ADHD is mostly a naming story, not a medical split. The condition is ADHD, and "ADD" is just the old word for its quieter, inattentive form. What matters is not the label, but whether your symptoms are getting in the way of the life you want.
