I've landed on an **ADHD focus timer** that actually sticks: 10 minutes of focused work, 3 minutes of rest, repeated on a loop, with my smartwatch buzzing on my wrist instead of a beep or chime telling me when to switch. No app open. No sound. Just a tap on my wrist that says "okay, move now."

This isn't the standard 25/5 Pomodoro technique. I shortened the work block and dropped the alarm sound entirely, and both changes turned out to matter more than I expected.

**Pro-tip from ADHD m8**: If you've ever set a Pomodoro timer, gotten annoyed at the ringing, and just... turned it off mid-task, this post is for you. That was me, for years.

---

### What the 10-3 Method Actually Is

The 10-3 method is a work-rest interval timer set to 10 minutes of focused work followed by 3 minutes of rest, repeated in a loop, using a smartwatch's vibration motor instead of an audible alarm to signal the switch. You set it once on your watch, put your phone away, and let your wrist do the interrupting.

The mechanics are simple:

- **10 minutes of work.** One task, phone face-down, no tab-switching.
- **Vibration.** The watch buzzes. That's the entire signal — no sound, no notification banner to read.
- **3 minutes of rest.** Stand up, stretch, get water, stare out a window. Anything except starting another task.
- **Vibration again.** Back to work for another 10.

It's a rhythm you feel more than a schedule you watch.

---

### Why 10 Minutes Works Better Than 25 for ADHD

Ten-minute work blocks work better for ADHD brains than the classic 25-minute Pomodoro because they sit inside a window most ADHD brains can actually sustain effortful attention for, rather than the window neurotypical time-management systems were designed around. The standard Pomodoro technique wasn't built with ADHD in mind — it assumes attention is a resource you spend down evenly. ADHD attention doesn't work that way; it's inconsistent, task-dependent, and prone to dropping off a cliff well before the 25-minute mark on anything that isn't inherently interesting.

Ten minutes is short enough that starting doesn't feel like a big commitment, which matters because **task initiation**, not sustained focus, is usually the actual barrier for ADHD brains. A 10-minute block is easy to say yes to even when a 25-minute one feels impossible. And because the block ends before attention has fully collapsed, you finish each sprint on a high note instead of dragging yourself through the last third of it.

The 3-minute rest matters just as much as the work block. It's long enough to genuinely reset — stand, move, look away from the screen — but short enough that momentum doesn't die. A 3-minute break rarely turns into a 20-minute scroll spiral the way a 5- or 10-minute break can, especially if you keep your phone out of the loop entirely (see below).

### Why Vibration Beats Sound for ADHD Timers

A vibrating alert works better than an audible alarm for ADHD focus timers because it interrupts without triggering the startle response or breaking hyperfocus loudly enough to derail the task you were just in. Anyone with ADHD who has been mid-flow on a task and gotten blasted by a timer alarm knows the specific irritation of it — the sound doesn't just tell you time's up, it yanks you out of whatever cognitive state you were in, often hard enough that getting back into a similar state for the next block takes longer than it should.

A wrist buzz doesn't do that. It's felt, not heard, which means it doesn't compete with music, a podcast, or background noise the way an alarm does, and it doesn't announce itself to anyone else in the room. **Vibrating watches like the WatchMinder** are already a recognized ADHD accommodation tool for exactly this reason — see our [full roundup of ADHD gadgets and tools](/posts/gadgets-and-tools-to-support-the-adhd-brain/) for other options in the same category. You don't need a specialty device, though. Most smartwatches on the market today can be configured to do this with a basic timer or workout interval app.

### How to Set This Up on Your Own Smartwatch

You don't need a specific brand of smartwatch to run the 10-3 method — any watch with a repeating interval timer or workout interval feature and vibration alerts (haptics) turned on will do the job. The exact menu path differs by device, but the setup logic is the same everywhere:

- **Find the interval or workout timer feature**, not the basic single-countdown timer. Look for "Intervals," "HIIT," or "Custom Workout" in the fitness app — most watches already have this built for exercise, and it repeats automatically without you resetting it every 10 minutes.
- **Set work interval to 10 minutes, rest interval to 3 minutes**, and set the number of rounds high enough to cover a work session (6 rounds covers roughly 78 minutes).
- **Turn off sound and turn on vibration-only alerts** in the watch's notification or alarm settings. This is the step people skip, and it's the one that makes the whole thing tolerable.
- **Silence phone notifications for the duration**, or leave the phone in another room. The watch is doing the timekeeping now; the phone re-entering the loop defeats the purpose.

If your watch doesn't support custom intervals, a basic repeating timer app set to alternate 10-minute and 3-minute countdowns, with vibration-only alerts, achieves the same effect.

### What to Do (and Not Do) During the 3-Minute Rest

The 3-minute rest works best when it's spent on something physical and screen-free rather than something that opens a new attention loop. Standing up, refilling water, stretching, or stepping outside for fresh air all reset attention without introducing a new hook to get pulled into. Checking your phone, opening a browser tab "just to look something up," or starting a second small task are the three most common ways a 3-minute rest quietly becomes a 20-minute one — and once that happens, the whole rhythm of the method breaks, because you come back to the next work block already scattered instead of reset.

If you're using this alongside **body doubling** — working near someone else, in person or on a video call — the rest period is also a natural moment to briefly check in ("what are you working on next?") without derailing either of you. ### Key Takeaways

- The **10-3 method** uses 10-minute work sprints and 3-minute rests on a repeating loop, signaled by smartwatch vibration instead of sound.
- Ten-minute blocks match the attention window many ADHD brains can sustain, and lower the barrier to task initiation compared to the standard 25-minute Pomodoro block.
- **Vibration-only alerts** interrupt focus more gently than audible alarms, avoiding the startle response that can derail hyperfocus.
- Set this up using your smartwatch's **interval or workout timer feature** with haptics on and sound off — no specialty ADHD device required.
- Keep the 3-minute rest physical and screen-free to avoid it turning into a longer, harder-to-recover-from break.

---

### Conclusion

The 10-3 method isn't magic, and it won't work identically for every ADHD brain — some people will find 10 minutes still too long on a hard day, or too short on a good one, and that's fine to adjust. What makes it worth trying is how little it asks of you to start: no app to babysit, no alarm to brace for, just a buzz on your wrist telling you it's time to move. For a brain that already fights task initiation and sensory overload, cutting the friction and the noise out of a timer is most of the win.

**Next step**: If you've got a smartwatch already sitting in a drawer, dig it out and set a 10-minute/3-minute interval before you try anything else. You'll know within one or two rounds whether the rhythm fits.
