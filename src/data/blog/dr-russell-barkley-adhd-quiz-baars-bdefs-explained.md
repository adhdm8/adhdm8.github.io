If you've searched for the **Dr. Russell Barkley ADHD quiz**, you've probably landed on one of a dozen unofficial "Barkley-inspired" quizzes scattered around the internet, and wondered whether any of them are the real thing. They mostly aren't. Barkley, one of the most cited ADHD researchers alive, built two formal, published clinical rating scales, not a viral internet quiz, and understanding what those scales actually measure is far more useful than taking a knockoff version of one.

This post breaks down what the **Barkley Adult ADHD Rating Scale (BAARS-IV)** and the **Barkley Deficits in Executive Functioning Scale (BDEFS)** actually assess, why Barkley built a second scale on top of the standard symptom checklist, and how you can reflect on the same framework yourself even though the official tools aren't free to self-administer.

**Pro-tip from ADHD m8**: If a website is offering you a free, instant "official Barkley ADHD test" with a score and a diagnosis-style verdict, that's not the real BAARS-IV or BDEFS. Both are commercially published, copyrighted instruments sold through Guilford Press, typically used by clinicians and researchers.

### Who Is Russell Barkley, and What Is the "Barkley ADHD Quiz" People Are Looking For?

Russell Barkley is a clinical psychologist and ADHD researcher whose work over four decades has shaped how the field understands ADHD as a disorder of **executive function and self-regulation**, not just inattention and hyperactivity. When people search for "the Barkley ADHD quiz," they're usually looking for one of two things he actually created: the BAARS-IV, a DSM-aligned symptom rating scale, or the BDEFS, a separate scale built specifically to measure executive function deficits in daily life.

Neither one is a single "quiz" in the BuzzFeed sense. Both are structured, validated rating scales designed for clinical and research use, which is a meaningfully different thing from a free online screener you complete for fun.

---

### The Barkley Adult ADHD Rating Scale (BAARS-IV): What It Measures

The **BAARS-IV** is a self-report and other-report rating scale that screens ADHD symptoms across three domains: inattention, hyperactivity/impulsivity, and **sluggish cognitive tempo (SCT)**. The self-report current-symptoms version runs to roughly 30 items, most rated on a 4-point frequency scale from "never or rarely" to "very often," and covers the nine inattention and six hyperactivity/impulsivity symptoms drawn from the DSM criteria, plus nine additional items assessing SCT.

That third domain is what sets it apart from most free adult ADHD screeners. **Sluggish cognitive tempo** describes symptoms like daydreaming, mental fogginess, staring off, and a general slow, sluggish mental pace, distinct from the more commonly recognized inattentive and hyperactive symptom clusters. Standard screeners like the ASRS-6 don't touch this domain at all, which is one reason some adults who feel "foggy" rather than "hyperactive" don't recognize themselves in a typical quiz. If you've taken our [ASRS-6 based ADHD quiz for adults](/posts/adhd-quiz-for-adults-self-screening-test/) and felt like it didn't quite capture your experience, SCT symptoms are often the missing piece.

The BAARS-IV also includes an other-report form, filled out by a spouse, parent, or close friend, because Barkley's research consistently found that adults with ADHD tend to underreport their own symptoms compared to how people close to them describe their behavior.

### Why Barkley Built a Second Scale: The BDEFS

The **Barkley Deficits in Executive Functioning Scale (BDEFS)** exists because Barkley's research found that a person's real-world impairment, missed deadlines, strained relationships, financial trouble, correlates more strongly with executive function deficits in daily life than with a raw DSM symptom count. Two adults can score identically on a symptom checklist and have very different levels of functional struggle; the BDEFS was built to capture that gap.

The scale organizes executive function into five factors, each covering a distinct facet of daily self-regulation:

- **Self-management to time** — planning ahead, estimating how long tasks take, and meeting deadlines.
- **Self-organization and problem-solving** — breaking a goal into steps and holding a plan together long enough to execute it.
- **Self-restraint** — inhibiting impulsive reactions, interruptions, and rash decisions.
- **Self-motivation** — generating the internal drive to start and finish tasks that aren't immediately rewarding.
- **Self-regulation of emotion** — managing frustration, disappointment, and emotional reactivity without it derailing behavior.

If **time blindness** sounds familiar, that maps directly onto the "self-management to time" factor. We go deeper on why that specific deficit happens in our piece on [the science of time blindness](/posts/the-mystery-of-time-blindness-why-the-adhd-brain-struggles-with-the-future/).

### Is There a Free Version of the Barkley ADHD Quiz You Can Take Online?

No, not a legitimate one. Both the BAARS-IV and the BDEFS are copyrighted, commercially published assessment instruments, sold as scoring kits through Guilford Press and psychological test publishers, and they're intended to be administered and interpreted by a clinician or researcher, not scored casually online. Any site offering an "official Barkley ADHD test" for free with an instant score is, at best, a paraphrased imitation, and at worst, a lead-generation quiz with no real connection to Barkley's published work.

That doesn't mean the *framework* is useless to you without paying for the scale. You can still reflect honestly on the same five executive function domains the BDEFS was built around; you just won't get a validated, normed score out of it the way a clinician administering the real instrument would.

### A Self-Reflection Checklist Inspired by Barkley's Five EF Domains

This isn't the official BDEFS, and it won't produce a clinical score, but it uses the same five-domain structure to help you think through where your own executive function struggles concentrate. For each domain, ask yourself honestly whether it's a persistent, daily pattern rather than an occasional bad week:

- **Time**: Do you regularly misjudge how long tasks will take, in either direction, and does this cause real friction (late fees, missed appointments, all-nighters)?
- **Organization/problem-solving**: Do multi-step tasks stall out because you lose the thread of the plan, even when you understood it clearly at the start?
- **Restraint**: Do you interrupt, blurt, or make quick decisions you regret, more often than the people around you seem to?
- **Motivation**: Do you know exactly what needs doing, and still can't make yourself start until a deadline creates real pressure?
- **Emotion**: Does frustration or disappointment tend to hit harder and last longer for you than it seems to for others, in a way that affects your decisions afterward?

If most of these feel like a persistent "yes" rather than an occasional one, that's a meaningful pattern worth bringing to a clinician, framed in exactly these terms. Executive function language like this is often more useful in an evaluation than a generic "I think I have ADHD."

### How the Barkley Scales Differ From the Quizzes You'll Find Elsewhere Online

Most free "ADHD quiz for adults" tools you'll find online, including the WHO-developed ASRS-6, are built to screen for the presence of DSM symptom criteria. Barkley's scales do that too with the BAARS-IV, but they go a step further with the BDEFS by measuring functional impairment in daily executive function, which is arguably closer to what actually disrupts a person's life. A high symptom count with low functional impairment and a moderate symptom count with severe functional impairment can look very different day-to-day, even though a basic checklist might treat them similarly.

That's a useful distinction to bring into any conversation with a clinician: it's not just "how many symptoms do I have," but "how much is this actually costing me day to day." Our [Adult ADHD Diagnosis Guide](/posts/adult-adhd-diagnosis-guide/) walks through what a full evaluation covers, and a good clinician will typically be assessing exactly this kind of functional impairment, not just running through a checklist.

### Key Takeaways

- The "**Dr. Russell Barkley ADHD quiz**" people search for usually refers to one of two real clinical tools: the **BAARS-IV** (symptom rating scale) or the **BDEFS** (executive function rating scale).
- The BAARS-IV adds a **sluggish cognitive tempo** domain that standard screeners like the ASRS-6 don't measure.
- The BDEFS is built around **five executive function domains**: time management, organization/problem-solving, restraint, motivation, and emotional regulation.
- Neither scale has a legitimate free version online — both are copyrighted, clinician-administered instruments, not casual internet quizzes.
- You can still use the five-domain framework as a genuine, if unofficial, self-reflection tool to identify patterns worth discussing with a clinician.

---

### Conclusion

The real value in Barkley's work isn't a quiz you can screenshot your score from. It's the reframing underneath both scales: ADHD in adulthood is as much about executive function breaking down in daily life as it is about a fixed list of symptoms. If a free quick screener like the ASRS-6 undersells how much your day-to-day is actually affected, thinking in terms of Barkley's five EF domains is often the missing vocabulary for describing it accurately, to yourself and to a clinician.
