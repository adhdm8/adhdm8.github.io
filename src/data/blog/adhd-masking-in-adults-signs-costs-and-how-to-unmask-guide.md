**ADHD masking in adults** means hiding or overriding your natural ADHD traits so you look organised, calm and "normal" to other people. It works well enough that many adults go undiagnosed for decades, and it quietly costs a huge amount of energy.

This guide covers the signs of ADHD masking, why people do it, what it costs, and how to start unmasking safely. If you've ever come home from a perfectly fine day feeling like you ran a marathon, keep reading.

> **Pro-tip from ADHD m8:** Masking is a survival skill, not a character flaw. You learned it for good reasons. The goal isn't to feel ashamed of it, just to stop paying for it at full price.

_This post is general information, not medical advice or a diagnosis. Talk to a qualified clinician about your own situation._

---

### What Is ADHD Masking?

ADHD masking is the conscious or automatic suppression of ADHD behaviours, and the creation of workarounds, to avoid judgement or consequences. It is sometimes called camouflaging or compensating.

Research on masking is much more developed in autism than in ADHD. A well-known [study of camouflaging by Hull and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Hull+camouflaging+autism+2017) describes hiding difficulties and compensating in social situations. Evidence specific to ADHD is still growing, but many adults describe the same pattern of high effort and invisible cost.

Masking is not the same as coping. Coping strategies, like using a calendar, reduce the load. Masking means acting out a version of yourself, which adds load.

---

### Signs of ADHD Masking in Adults

The signs of ADHD masking are mostly invisible to others, which is exactly the point. Common examples include:

- **Over-preparing**: scripting conversations or rehearsing meetings in your head
- **Forcing stillness**: suppressing fidgeting, then paying for it later with exhaustion
- **Faking attention**: nodding along and making eye contact while your mind has left the building
- **Hiding mistakes**: quietly fixing errors at night so nobody sees them
- **Perfectionism and people-pleasing**: saying yes to everything so nobody sees you struggle
- **Crashing at home**: holding it together all day, then melting down once you are alone

If several of these feel uncomfortably familiar, you may be masking more than you realise.

---

### Why Do Adults With ADHD Mask?

Adults with ADHD mask because they learned early that their natural behaviour was criticised, punished or misread as laziness. Hiding it became the safest option.

Many people heard "try harder" or "you're so careless" for years. That history often goes hand in hand with [rejection sensitive dysphoria](/posts/rejection-sensitive-dysphoria-adhd-symptoms-and-coping-guide), where the fear of disapproval feels physically painful. Masking then becomes a way to dodge that pain before it starts.

### Who Masks Most? Women, Late Diagnosis and ADHD

Women and people with inattentive ADHD are often described as the most likely to mask, and this is one reason ADHD is diagnosed later in them. Research on women with ADHD, such as [Quinn and Madhoo's review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Quinn+Madhoo+women+ADHD+2014), notes that symptoms are often internalised and mistaken for anxiety or depression.

Anyone can mask, though. Men, high achievers and people raised in strict or high-pressure environments do it too. For a deeper look at screening, see our [ADHD test for women guide](/posts/adhd-test-for-women-self-screening-guide) and our [adult ADHD diagnosis guide](/posts/adult-adhd-diagnosis-guide).

### The Hidden Cost of Masking ADHD

The cost of masking ADHD is sustained mental effort, which can contribute to exhaustion, anxiety and a weaker sense of who you actually are. You are running a full-time performance on top of your real job.

Possible costs include:

- **Burnout**: constant self-monitoring drains the same executive functions ADHD already taxes. Our [ADHD burnout guide](/posts/adhd-burnout-symptoms-and-recovery-guide) explains the crash.
- **Emotional blow-ups**: held-in feelings tend to leak out later. See [ADHD emotional dysregulation](/posts/adhd-emotional-dysregulation-adults-guide).
- **Delayed support**: if you look fine, nobody offers help, and you may not ask.
- **Identity fog**: after years of acting, it can be hard to tell what you actually like.

Note that research linking masking to specific outcomes in ADHD is still limited, so treat these as common reports, not proven rules.

### How to Start Unmasking Safely

Unmasking means gradually dropping the masking behaviours that cost you the most, in places where it is safe to do so. It is a dial, not a switch, and it doesn't mean announcing everything to everyone.

#### Step 1: Notice the pattern

For one week, jot down when you feel most "on" and when you crash. A quick note on your phone is enough. You are collecting data, not judging yourself.

#### Step 2: Pick one low-risk place to ease off

Choose a safe person or setting, such as a close friend, a partner or your own home. Try fidgeting openly, saying "I lost my train of thought, can you repeat that?" or skipping a performance you don't need.

#### Step 3: Replace masks with tools

Swap hidden effort for open systems: reminders, body doubling, visual timers and written agendas. These reduce load instead of adding it. Our [body doubling guide](/posts/body-doubling-for-adhd-best-apps-and-how-it-works) is a good place to start.

#### Step 4: Ask for adjustments at work

If you are comfortable, request practical changes like written instructions or flexible hours. Our [workplace accommodations guide](/posts/adhd-workplace-accommodations-guide) lists realistic options.

> **Pro-tip from ADHD m8:** You don't have to disclose a diagnosis to ask for what helps. "I work best with a written summary after meetings" is a perfectly normal sentence.

#### Step 5: Get professional support

A clinician or ADHD coach can help you work out what is masking, what is genuine preference, and whether other conditions are involved. The [NIMH overview of ADHD](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd) and [CHADD](https://chadd.org) are reliable starting points.

### FAQ: ADHD Masking

**Q:** Is ADHD masking a formal diagnosis?

**A:** No. Masking is a descriptive term for a pattern of behaviour, not a diagnosis, and it doesn't appear as a criterion in diagnostic manuals.

**Q:** Can masking stop me getting diagnosed?

**A:** It can. If you hide symptoms well, clinicians may see fewer visible signs. Tell your assessor about the effort, the crashes and your history, not just how you look on the day.

**Q:** Is unmasking always a good idea?

**A:** Not in every setting. Unmask where it's safe and worth it, and keep some professional boundaries. Being selective is not the same as being fake.

### Key Takeaways

- ADHD masking is the hiding or overriding of ADHD traits to appear "normal", and it is a learned response to criticism, not a flaw.
- Common signs include over-preparing, forced stillness, hiding mistakes and crashing at home after holding it together.
- Women and people with inattentive ADHD are often described as masking more, which can delay diagnosis.
- Sustained masking can contribute to burnout and emotional overload, though ADHD-specific research is still developing.
- Unmasking works best gradually: notice the pattern, ease off in safe places, replace masks with tools, and seek support.

---

### Conclusion

ADHD masking helped you get through school, work and relationships, but it was never meant to run all day, every day. You can keep the parts that serve you and put down the parts that drain you.

**Next step**: This week, pick one safe person or place and drop one mask there, such as fidgeting freely or asking someone to repeat themselves.
